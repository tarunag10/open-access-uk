Law changed 1 May 2026

Section 21 no-fault evictions were abolished in England on 1 May 2026 under the Renters' Rights Act 2025. A pre-abolition Section 21 notice could only be used to start a court claim by 31 July 2026. That deadline has passed; claims issued by then may continue. From 1 May 2026, all possession claims use the revised Section 8 grounds with new mandatory grounds and a raised serious-arrears threshold (3+ months). For current guidance, visit GOV.UK guidance for landlords, the official assured tenancy forms, or Shelter.

Business tenancy renewal checklist

A preliminary checklist for business tenancy renewal notices in England and Wales under Part II of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1954. This is a separate process from residential possession. It does not generate or replace prescribed forms.

Use the current prescribed notice forms and the GOV.UK business lease renewal guide. Guidance updated 30 July 2026.

Check the lease and any warning notice, declaration or agreement made before the lease was granted. This form cannot establish whether contracting out was legally effective.

Other prescribed forms may apply to special statutory circumstances. In particular, Form 7 may be relevant where the tenant may have rights under the Leasehold Reform Act 1967. The checklist does not assess eligibility for those forms.

Grounds include tenant breaches, persistent delay in paying rent, other substantial breaches or suitable alternative accommodation, and landlord redevelopment or own occupation. Their application depends on detailed facts and evidence. See section 30.

If there were additional extensions, record and check the full sequence. This checklist compares one further extension only. Each further agreement must be made before the then-current period expires.

The special First-tier Tribunal and Upper Tribunal jurisdiction applies only if both statutory conditions are met. Check the agreement and code rights; these answers do not determine jurisdiction.

Application deadline (preliminary estimate)

  • Section 25: the statutory period ends on the termination date stated in the notice.
  • Section 26: the statutory period ends immediately before the proposed new tenancy date. A tenant’s Section 24(1) application generally cannot be made until two months after the request unless the landlord has given a Section 26(6) counter-notice.
  • Section 29B can extend the application period by agreement. Check the timing and terms of every agreement.

Read the statutory wording in section 29A and section 29B, and the claim procedure in CPR Part 56 and Practice Direction 56.

Key procedure points

  • Section 25 and Section 26 are alternative ways to start the renewal timetable. Once one is served, the other route is generally unavailable for the same tenancy.
  • The proposed date and service date generally need to fall within a window of 6 to 12 months. Verify the lease date and prescribed form.
  • A landlord wishing to oppose a Section 26 request must generally serve a counter-notice within two months.
  • Applications must be started by the applicable statutory or agreed date. The result below is a preliminary date calculation, not confirmation that an application is valid, correctly issued or within time.

Before relying on a possession notice

Historic Section 21 cases

Private landlords cannot serve a new Section 21 notice. The deadline to start a court claim using a pre-1 May 2026 notice was 31 July 2026. Claims started by then may continue. For a live case, get advice on the notice and court papers.

Deposit protection rules

Your deposit must be protected in a government-approved scheme within 30 days of receiving it. The landlord must also serve prescribed information. Deposit rules affect most possession grounds. Check the current GOV.UK guidance.

Court process

If the landlord proceeds to court, you will receive a possession hearing date. You can defend the claim if the notice is invalid. Seek free legal advice from Shelter, Citizens Advice, or a housing solicitor.

Not legal advice

This is an educational tool, not legal advice. Verify all information against current UK housing law. Consult a qualified housing solicitor or contact Shelter for free housing advice.
